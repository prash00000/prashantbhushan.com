Too often, courtroom conversation treats fair-trial guarantees as a one-sided procedural shield. Yet constitutional protections under **Article 21** do not belong exclusively to the accused — they protect the victim who sets the legal process in motion and endures years of adjournments, travel, and uncertainty.

> A fair trial is not the accused's private privilege. The test isn't just whether justice was done, but whether the person who sought it survived the seeking.

## Why Distance and Delay Erode Substantive Justice

When proceedings drag across jurisdictions or stall on routine procedural defaults, the cost falls asymmetrically on complainants and witnesses with limited institutional support:

- **Economic attrition:** Repeated appearances across distant districts turn statutory remedies into an endurance test.
- **Evidentiary decay:** Memory fades and witnesses disengage long before arguments on merits begin.
- **Erosion of deterrence:** When accountability arrives years after harm, the law signals toleration rather than protection.

## Station CCTV and Habeas Corpus Accountability

The same institutional tension shows up at the threshold of liberty. In a recent Allahabad High Court habeas corpus hearing before Justices Atul Sreedharan and Achal Sachdev concerning allegations of unlawful detention prior to the recorded arrest date, the Court sharply criticized the repeated failure to produce police station CCTV footage when summoned.

When primary records like station CCTV disappear or go unproduced, courts are forced to reconstruct basic custody timelines after the fact. Electronic preservation is not a technical nicety — it is basic proof of compliance with constitutional arrest safeguards.

## The Practical Takeaway

Whether evaluating trial delay or custody records, Article 21 turns on verifiable timelines: who was detained when, why a hearing stalled, and whether procedure served real justice rather than delay.
